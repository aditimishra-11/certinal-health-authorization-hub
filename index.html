DPDP exposure

Every entity. Same four breakages. The permission was given — nothing executed it.

The DPDP Rules are now final — notified Nov 2025, with hospitals required to comply by ~May 2027 and penalties up to ₹250 crore. India also gives no GDPR-style ‘legitimate interest’ shortcut, so for anything beyond your care, consent isn’t optional. The clock is running on closing these gaps.

THE REFRAME

Healthcare does not have a consent problem.

Healthcare has a permission execution problem.

Capturing a signature is easy. Proving who was allowed to do what, then executing it across systems — that’s the unsolved part.

Product Overview

Four modules.
One system of record.

Every permission goes through the same four steps — captured, enforced, withdrawn, and proven.

System of record · consent
Module 01

Consent Lifecycle

Every permission captured, versioned, and time-bound — from first signature to expiry.

  • Multi-channel capture: in-app, kiosk, paper-to-digital, and ABDM-linked.
  • Purpose-scoped grants with explicit validity windows and data categories.
  • Immutable version history — every re-consent is a new signed record.
  • DPDP-aligned notices in the patient's language.
Real-time enforcement
Module 02

Authorization Workflow Engine

Turns a stored permission into a live gate on every data request — no consent, no access.

  • Policy-as-code rules map each purpose to who may act on what data.
  • Real-time allow / deny decisions at the API edge, in milliseconds.
  • Visual workflow builder for multi-party approvals and step-up checks.
  • Drop-in SDK / API for EMR, HIS, and ABDM gateways.
Revoke & propagate
Module 03

Withdrawal Management

One tap to revoke — and the withdrawal cascades to every downstream system that was relying on it.

  • Patient-initiated withdrawal from any channel, honored instantly.
  • Cascading revocation notifies every integrated system and processor.
  • Confirmed-stop receipts close the loop on each downstream halt.
  • Partial withdrawal — pull one purpose without breaking active care.
Tamper-evident ledger
Module 04

Audit Evidence Vault

Every grant, access, and revocation written to an immutable ledger — regulator-ready in one click.

  • Cryptographically chained, tamper-evident event log.
  • Reconstruct who accessed what, under which consent, at any moment.
  • One-click DPDP / audit export with full chain of custody.
  • Breach-ready timeline that proves exactly what was authorized.
MODULE 01

Consent Lifecycle

Capture purpose-based consent in the patient's own language, signed and version-tracked — then keep every version in a queryable repository. This is data-use consent: treating you runs on a legitimate-use notice, and the opt-ins are secondary uses like insurance, research and external sharing — each scoped to the visit.

Two kinds of sharing, two rules. Sharing to help you — care, referrals — you just get told. Sharing to help the business — insurance, research, marketing — you must be asked, and you can take it back. The Hub tells them apart — and proves which one happened.

PROTOTYPE JOURNEY 01

Consent in the OPD waiting room.

Most patients meet consent on a busy outpatient morning — standing in a crowded queue, maybe reading slowly, maybe not in English. So I design for that first, not last.

A front-desk staffer drives the screen on a shared kiosk. The patient watches, listens, and chooses.
Plain words, big buttons, the patient's own language, and a read-aloud option — not legal walls of text.
If the patient says no to something required, care isn't silently blocked — the refusal is captured and routed to a clinician.

ABHA = Ayushman Bharat Health Account, India's national health ID. Consent purpose = a specific data use you opt into (e.g. sharing with your insurer, or research). Treating you is a legitimate use shown as notice — not a toggle. Under DPDP, each secondary purpose needs its own clear yes.

    SCOPE & SEQUENCING

    What I’d build now —
    and what I’m not building (yet).

    A focused execution layer beats a sprawling platform. The MVP ships the four modules that make permissions executable. Everything else is sequenced — on purpose.

    The committed core — v1

    Four modules. One promise: every patient permission becomes an auditable, enforceable workflow.

    Building now
    01

    Consent Lifecycle

    Capture, version, and prove consent across channels — with purpose binding and OTP-verified provenance.

    Committed
    02

    Authorization Engine

    Turn a consent into live, scoped grants. Time-boxed, purpose-locked access that downstream systems must honor.

    Committed
    03

    Withdrawal Management

    One-tap revoke that propagates in seconds — with downstream purge receipts, not promises.

    Committed
    04

    Audit Vault

    An immutable, hash-chained ledger. Any permission history exported as a sealed, regulator-ready package in seconds.

    Committed
    These aren’t missing features — they’re sequenced bets. Each is a natural extension once the execution core has live permission data to act on.

    Deliberately deferred

    Adjacent capabilities the core unlocks — shipped only after the execution layer earns the right to expand.

    On the roadmap

    DSAR Automation

    Self-serve subject access requests, fulfilled from the audit ledger.

    Phase 2

    Data Retention

    Policy-driven expiry & deletion scheduling tied to grant lifecycles.

    Phase 2

    Data Discovery

    Map where permissioned data actually lives across systems.

    Phase 3

    Vendor Governance

    Track which processors hold which grants, with attestations.

    Phase 3

    AI Governance

    Consent-aware guardrails for models trained on health data.

    Exploring

    Cookie Consent

    Web-tier consent banners — the commoditized layer, last.

    Exploring
    ANTI-GOALS What I’m explicitly not building.
    Not a OneTrust clone
    Generic privacy suites optimize for breadth. It optimizes for healthcare permission execution — depth where it matters.
    Not an EMR or ABDM replacement
    It doesn’t store records or move clinical data. It sits beside them as the layer that enforces who’s allowed to.
